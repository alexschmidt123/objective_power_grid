\section{Methodology}

\subsection{Formulation Workflow and Decision Problem}

Let
\[
\theta=(\mathbf M,\mathbf K)\in\Theta
\]
denote the uncertain inertia and fast frequency-response parameters of an
IEEE test-system dynamic model. The study considers the IEEE 9- and 14-bus
systems; the network model and uncertainty distribution are fixed
within each comparison. The experiment consists of a fixed number \(T\) of
sequential probe tests. At probe step \(t\), an adaptive policy \(\pi\) selects
\[
\xi_t=\pi_t(h_{t-1}),
\]
where
\[
h_{t-1}
=
\bigl(
(\xi_1,\mathbf y_{\mathrm{obs},1}),
\ldots,
(\xi_{t-1},\mathbf y_{\mathrm{obs},t-1})
\bigr)
\]
is the available experimental history. Because \(T\) is identical for all policies, the probe budget is imposed as a constraint rather than included as a cost term.

The terminal engineering decision is a scalar supplementary active-power
injection whose application location, activation time, and temporal profile are
fixed; only its magnitude \(u\in\mathcal S\) is optimized. That magnitude is
selected to protect a prespecified set \(\mathcal C\) of credible terminal
contingencies rather than one specially chosen disturbance. The probe signals
\(\xi_t\) are small diagnostic tests and are not themselves the risky
operational intervention. A less informative probe remains admissible but may
require a more conservative terminal action.

Following Yoon et al.~\cite{yoon2013}, the terminal quantity of interest is a
robust operator \(\psi^\star(h_T)\) chosen from a one-parameter family of support
injections; its magnitude is written into the swing equation as
\(u_{\mathrm{ctrl}}(h_T)=\psi^\star(h_T)\). The methodological objective is to
identify a sequential probe strategy that minimizes the \emph{mean objective
cost of uncertainty} (MOCU), i.e.\ the posterior expectation of the
\emph{objective cost of uncertainty} (OCU) of that robust operator relative to
the model-specific optimum. The resulting decision chain is
\[
\boxed{
\pi
\longrightarrow
\xi_{1:T}
\longrightarrow
\mathbf y_{\mathrm{obs},1:T}
\longrightarrow
p(\theta\mid h_T)
\longrightarrow
\psi^\star(h_T)
=
u_{\mathrm{ctrl}}(h_T).
}
\]
The presentation follows the causal workflow used by the implementation:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item specify the power-grid dynamics and latent $\mathbf M,\mathbf K$ space;
  \item specify reset-to-equilibrium probe actions and noisy observations;
  \item update the posterior and define parameter EIG;
  \item define terminal $u_{\mathrm{ctrl}}$ and the frequency-security criteria;
  \item construct control-oriented MOCU from the minimum safe control cost; and
  \item optimize and evaluate sequential probe-selection policies.
\end{enumerate}
This ordering separates the physical model, statistical experiment, and
operational objective before introducing any learning algorithm.

\paragraph{Scientific question.}
The study asks whether a policy that plans over the remaining probe budget can
reduce terminal control uncertainty beyond a strong one-step control-oriented
policy. This distinction is essential: DAD and RL-sBOED are not expected to
outperform Myopic when the one-step objective is effectively submodular, when
one probe nearly resolves the control-relevant uncertainty, or when training
collapses to an open-loop sequence. A defensible advantage must instead arise
from observable history-dependent branching or complementary probes whose
joint terminal value is not captured by their immediate one-step values.
Accordingly, the experiments test for non-myopic opportunity rather than
assuming it.

\subsection{1. Power-Grid Dynamics and Latent Parameter Space}

The probe construction follows the physical frequency-response model used by Peng et al.~\cite{peng2024}. At the aggregate level,
\[
\frac{2HS_n}{\omega_s}
\frac{d\Delta\omega(t)}{dt}
=
-\Delta P_e(t)
-
\left(
\frac{K}{2\pi}+D
\right)
\Delta\omega(t),
\]
where \(H\) is the inertia constant, \(S_n\) is the system power base, \(\omega_s\) is synchronous angular speed, \(K\) is the aggregate fast frequency-response coefficient, \(D\) is damping, and
\[
\Delta\omega(t)=\omega(t)-\omega(0).
\]
Defining
\[
M=\frac{2HS_n}{\omega_s},
\]
the aggregate response equation becomes
\[
\boxed{
M\frac{d\Delta\omega(t)}{dt}
=
-\Delta P_e(t)
-
\left(
\frac{K}{2\pi}+D
\right)
\Delta\omega(t).
}
\label{eq:aggregate_model}
\]

Equation~\eqref{eq:aggregate_model} provides the physical basis for the probe test. In the present study, the corresponding IEEE test-system simulator generates the bus-level frequency responses and determines how the design location and the parameter vector \(\theta=(\mathbf M,\mathbf K)\) affect the observation.

\subsection{2. Reset Probe Experiment, Design Space, and Observation}

A probe action is the bus--duration pair
\[
\xi_t=(b_t,d_t)\in\Xi=\mathcal B_{\mathrm p}\times\mathcal D,
\qquad N_\xi=|\mathcal B_{\mathrm p}|\,|\mathcal D|,
\label{eq:design_space}
\]
where the safe diagnostic amplitude $A$ is fixed across actions. The primary
IEEE9 and IEEE14 catalogs use every physical bus as a candidate probe location
and six prespecified durations, giving 54 and 84 actions, respectively. Exact
repetition of a bus--duration pair is prohibited within a rollout. The
sequential horizon $T$ is the probe budget. The injected Hann pulse is
\[
P_{\mathrm{probe}}(s;\xi_t)
=
\begin{cases}
\displaystyle
A\cos^2
\left[
\frac{\pi}{d_t}
\left(
s-\frac{d_t}{2}
\right)
\right],
&
0\le s\le d_t,
\\[1.1em]
0,
&
s>d_t.
\end{cases}
\label{eq:hann_probe}
\]
For the aggregate model, a positive probe injection gives
\[
\Delta P_e(s)=-P_{\mathrm{probe}}(s;\xi_t),
\]
and therefore
\[
M\frac{d\Delta\omega^{\,p}(s)}{ds}
=
P_{\mathrm{probe}}(s;\xi_t)
-
\left(
\frac{K}{2\pi}+D
\right)
\Delta\omega^{\,p}(s).
\label{eq:probe_model}
\]

The probe and terminal control are evaluated in separate simulations. During every probe simulation,
\[
u_{\mathrm{ctrl}}=0.
\]
In the primary continuous-duration track, every diagnostic probe starts from
the same equilibrium state. The physical response is therefore conditionally
independent of earlier probes given \(\theta\) and \(\xi_t\), while the design
policy remains adaptive through the accumulated belief,
\[
\mathbf y_{\mathrm{obs},t}
\longrightarrow
p(\theta\mid h_t)
\longrightarrow
\xi_{t+1},
\]
and no simulator call is made during training or evaluation: all clean
responses are read from the offline databank.

\subsubsection{Noisy frequency observation}

Let \(f_i^{\,p}(s;\theta,\xi_t)\) denote the simulated frequency at monitored
bus \(i\) during the independent probe \(\xi_t\). Define
\[
\Delta f_i^{\,p}(s;\theta,\xi_t)
=
f_i^{\,p}(s;\theta,\xi_t)-f_i^{\,p}(0;\theta,\xi_t).
\]
The simulator produces \(N_{\mathrm{sim}}\) time samples on the current probe
window. In the primary continuous-duration experiments the method-visible
observation is a fixed subsample of the frequency-deviation trajectory at the
probe bus. For configurable observation dimension \(N_{\mathrm{obs}}\)
and sampling indices \(q_1,\ldots,q_{N_{\mathrm{obs}}}\),
\[
\boxed{
\mathbf y(\theta,\xi_t)
=
\left[
\Delta f_b^{\,p}(s_{q_1};\theta,\xi_t),\ldots,
\Delta f_b^{\,p}(s_{q_{N_{\mathrm{obs}}}};\theta,\xi_t)
\right]^{\!\top},
}
\label{eq:noise_free_response}
\]
and the measured vector is
\[
\boxed{
\mathbf y_t
=
\mathbf y(\theta^\star,\xi_t)
+
\boldsymbol\epsilon_t,
\qquad
\boldsymbol\epsilon_t
\sim
\mathcal N\!\left(\mathbf 0,
\sigma_y^2\mathbf I_{N_{\mathrm{obs}}}\right).
}
\label{eq:observation}
\]
The physical bank retains the full clean \(\Delta f\) trajectory and scalar
maximum absolute ROCOF. Thus \(N_{\mathrm{obs}}\) and \(\sigma_y\) can be selected
by a validation-only information-saturation audit without regenerating physics.

\subsection{3. Bayesian Belief Update and Expected Information Gain}

The likelihood for probe \(t\) under the sampled-trajectory observation is
\[
p(\mathbf y_t\mid\theta,\xi_t)
=
\mathcal N
\left(
\mathbf y_t;
\mathbf y(\theta,\xi_t),
\sigma_y^2\mathbf I_{N_{\mathrm{obs}}}
\right).
\]
Because independent probes reset to the common equilibrium and observation
errors are conditionally independent, the posterior after \(T\) probes is
\[
\boxed{
p(\theta\mid h_T)
=
\frac{
p_0(\theta)
\displaystyle\prod_{t=1}^{T}
p(\mathbf y_t\mid\theta,\xi_t)
}{
\displaystyle
\int_{\Theta}
p_0(\vartheta)
\prod_{t=1}^{T}
p(\mathbf y_t\mid\vartheta,\xi_t)
\,d\vartheta
}.
}
\label{eq:posterior}
\]

With particles
\[
\left\{
\theta^{(n)},w_n^{(0)}
\right\}_{n=1}^{N},
\]
the posterior is approximated by
\[
p(\theta\mid h_t)
\approx
\sum_{n=1}^{N}
w_n^{(t)}
\delta_{\theta^{(n)}}(\theta),
\]
where
\[
\boxed{
w_n^{(t)}
=
\frac{
w_n^{(t-1)}
p(\mathbf y_t\mid\theta^{(n)},\xi_t)
}{
\displaystyle
\sum_{m=1}^{N}
w_m^{(t-1)}
p(\mathbf y_t\mid\theta^{(m)},\xi_t)
}.
}
\label{eq:weight_update}
\]

For the parameter-learning track, the utility of policy $\pi$ is the mutual
information between the latent grid parameters and the terminal history,
\[
\boxed{
\operatorname{EIG}(\pi)
=I(\theta;H_T\mid\pi)
=\mathbb E_{H_T\mid\pi}\left[
D_{\mathrm{KL}}\bigl(p(\theta\mid H_T)\,\|\,p_0(\theta)\bigr)
\right].
}
\label{eq:eig_objective}
\]
With a discrete particle posterior, the realized information gain is the
prior-to-posterior entropy reduction. EIG and MOCU are separate scientific
objectives: EIG rewards learning $\theta$, whereas MOCU rewards learning only
insofar as it improves the terminal operational decision.

\subsection{4. Terminal Control Action and Safety Conditions}

The terminal control calculation is performed after all \(T\) probes and is independent of the probe simulations. During this stage,
\[
P_{\mathrm{probe}}(s)=0.
\]

Let \(\mathcal C\) be a fixed, publicly specified set of credible disturbance
locations and magnitudes used by every method. For a hypothetical parameter
vector \(\theta\), contingency \(c\in\mathcal C\), and candidate control
magnitude \(u\), let
\[
r_{\mathrm{ctrl}}(\theta,c,u)
\]
denote the maximum absolute ROCOF obtained from the terminal control simulation, and let
\[
f_{\mathrm{nadir}}(\theta,c,u)
\]
denote the minimum frequency over the terminal control horizon. Define the
contingency-specific minimum safe magnitude by
\[
\boxed{
u_{\mathrm{req}}(\theta,c)
=
\min
\left\{
u\in\mathcal S:
r_{\mathrm{ctrl}}(\theta,c,u)\le r_{\max},
\quad
f_{\mathrm{nadir}}(\theta,c,u)\ge f_{\min}
\right\},
}
\label{eq:u_req}
\]
where \(\mathcal S\subset[0,\infty)\) is the admissible support-magnitude set.

\subsection{5. Control-Oriented MOCU}

The terminal decision must satisfy the frequency-security requirements while
using the least supplementary power. Safety is therefore treated as a
feasibility requirement, not as an additional weighted penalty. This produces
a single operational cost and permits direct use of the original OCU/MOCU
construction of Yoon et al.~\cite{yoon2013}.

\subsubsection{Safe control set}

For model parameter \(\theta\), define
\begin{equation}
\mathcal U_{\mathrm{safe}}(\theta)
=
\left\{
u\in\mathcal S:
\begin{array}{ll}
f_{\mathrm{nadir}}(\theta,c,u)\ge f_{\min}, & \forall c\in\mathcal C,\\
r_{\mathrm{ctrl}}(\theta,c,u)\le r_{\max}, & \forall c\in\mathcal C
\end{array}
\right\}.
\label{eq:safe_control_set}
\end{equation}
The admissible control grid \(\mathcal S=\{u^1<\cdots<u^{N_u}\}\), contingency
set \(\mathcal C\), and limits \(f_{\min}\) and \(r_{\max}\) are common to all
probe-selection methods. The configured grid is valid only if
\(\mathcal U_{\mathrm{safe}}(\theta)\ne\varnothing\) for every retained model.

\subsubsection{Model-specific and Bayesian controls}

Let \(J(u)\) denote the cost of applying control magnitude \(u\). The primary
experiments use \(J(u)=u\); a monetary cost may replace it if independently
available. If \(\theta\) were known, the optimal action would be
\begin{equation}
u_\theta^*
=\arg\min_{u\in\mathcal U_{\mathrm{safe}}(\theta)}J(u).
\label{eq:model_specific_control}
\end{equation}
Thus, for increasing \(J\), \(u_\theta^*\) is the minimum safe injection. It is
computed in the terminal-control simulations and stored once for every
particle; it is not inferred from a probe trajectory.

Let \(b(\theta)=p(\theta\mid h_T)\) be the posterior after the final probe. The
original Yoon intrinsically Bayesian robust action minimizes posterior expected
operational cost. Equivalently, introduce the constrained cost
\begin{equation}
L(\theta,u)=
\begin{cases}
J(u), & u\in\mathcal U_{\mathrm{safe}}(\theta),\\
+\infty, & u\notin\mathcal U_{\mathrm{safe}}(\theta),
\end{cases}
\label{eq:operational_cost}
\end{equation}
and define the practical terminal action by
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
u_{\mathrm{ctrl}}(b)
=\arg\min_{u\in\mathcal S}
\mathbb E_{\theta\sim b}[L(\theta,u)].
}
\label{eq:u_ctrl}
\end{equation}
For monotone safety and increasing \(J\), this is the smallest action that is
safe for every model in the posterior support,
\begin{equation}
u_{\mathrm{ctrl}}(b)
=\max_{\theta\in\operatorname{supp}(b)}u_\theta^*.
\label{eq:u_ctrl_support}
\end{equation}
Here \(u_{\mathrm{ctrl}}\) is the action applied to the grid, not a measure of
uncertainty.

\subsubsection{OCU and MOCU}

For a particular model, the objective cost of uncertainty is the excess cost
of using the Bayesian robust action instead of the action that would be chosen
if that model were known:
\begin{equation}
\operatorname{OCU}(\theta,b)
=L\!\left(\theta,u_{\mathrm{ctrl}}(b)\right)
-L(\theta,u_\theta^*).
\label{eq:ocu}
\end{equation}
Because \(u_{\mathrm{ctrl}}(b)\) is safe for every model in the posterior
support, this becomes
\begin{equation}
\operatorname{OCU}(\theta,b)
=J\!\left(u_{\mathrm{ctrl}}(b)\right)-J(u_\theta^*)\ge0.
\label{eq:ocu_control}
\end{equation}
The mean objective cost of uncertainty is its posterior expectation,
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\operatorname{MOCU}(b)
=J\!\left(u_{\mathrm{ctrl}}(b)\right)
-\mathbb E_{\theta\sim b}[J(u_\theta^*)].
}
\label{eq:belief_mocu}
\end{equation}
For \(J(u)=u\), MOCU is the expected excess injection required solely because
the true model is uncertain:
\begin{equation}
\operatorname{MOCU}(b)
=u_{\mathrm{ctrl}}(b)-\mathbb E_{\theta\sim b}[u_\theta^*].
\label{eq:belief_mocu_linear}
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Particle approximation}

For particles \(\{\theta^{(n)}\}_{n=1}^N\) with posterior weights \(w_n\), store
\(u_n^*=u_{\theta^{(n)}}^*\). Numerical posterior support is
\begin{equation}
\mathcal I(b)=\{n:w_n>\varepsilon_w\},
\qquad \varepsilon_w=10^{-12}.
\end{equation}
The particle approximation of the robust action and posterior MOCU is
\begin{align}
u_{\mathrm{ctrl}}(b)
&=\max_{n\in\mathcal I(b)}u_n^*,
\label{eq:particle_u_ctrl}\\
\operatorname{MOCU}(b)
&=J\!\left(u_{\mathrm{ctrl}}(b)\right)
-\sum_{n=1}^{N} w_n J(u_n^*).
\label{eq:particle_mocu}
\end{align}
The weights are normalized in log space, so \(\mathcal I(b)\) is nonempty.
Scientific alignment requires the same posterior support, operational cost,
contingency set, and safety limits in policy optimization and posterior
evaluation. Under this requirement, both stages represent the same terminal
decision problem.

\subsubsection{Sequential design objective and safety reporting}

The probe policy minimizes expected terminal MOCU,
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\pi^*=\arg\min_\pi
\mathbb E_{H_T\mid\pi}
\left[
\operatorname{MOCU}\!\left(p(\theta\mid H_T)\right)
\right].
}
\label{eq:mocu_policy}
\end{equation}
Equivalently, it maximizes expected prior-to-terminal MOCU reduction.

MOCU measures excess control cost among posterior-safe decisions; it does not
replace out-of-sample validation of security. Accordingly, terminal
\(u_{\mathrm{ctrl}}\), held-out safety probability, frequency-nadir violations,
and ROCOF violations are reported separately. Methods that fail the
prespecified held-out safety requirement are not ranked by MOCU. This separation
avoids introducing an unsupported numerical exchange rate between control
effort and safety.

\subsection{6. Policy Optimization, Feasibility, and Evaluation}

\subsubsection{Diagnostic-probe feasibility and terminal safety}

The probe stage and the terminal control stage have fundamentally different roles. Each probe is a small diagnostic perturbation used only to generate an informative observation. It is not evaluated as a candidate emergency control action, and no probe is rejected because it might imply a large terminal control requirement. During a probe simulation,
\[
u_{\mathrm{ctrl}}=0,
\]
whereas during a terminal control simulation,
\[
P_{\mathrm{probe}}(s)=0.
\]

The control candidate set is assumed to contain a sufficiently large value that protects every parameter value represented in the operational uncertainty set. Formally, for
\[
u_{\max}=\max\mathcal S,
\]
assume
\[
\boxed{
 r_{\mathrm{ctrl}}(\theta,c,u_{\max})\le r_{\max},
\qquad
 f_{\mathrm{nadir}}(\theta,c,u_{\max})\ge f_{\min},
\quad
 \forall(\theta,c)\in\Theta_{\mathrm{op}}\times\mathcal C.
}
\label{eq:control_feasibility}
\]
Under Eq.~\eqref{eq:control_feasibility}, every valid probe history admits a terminal control decision. A weak probe strategy is therefore not unsafe or infeasible; it simply tends to leave more uncertainty and may consequently produce a larger IBR operator \(\psi^\star=\max_n \psi_n^\star\). If Eq.~\eqref{eq:control_feasibility} fails for the configured control range, the control range is incomplete and must be expanded before comparing probe-selection methods.

\subsubsection{Control-oriented sequential design objective}

The probe-selection mechanism affects the terminal engineering action through
\[
\pi
\longrightarrow
\xi_{1:T}
\longrightarrow
h_T
\longrightarrow
p(\theta\mid h_T)
\longrightarrow
\psi^\star(h_T)
=
u_{\mathrm{ctrl}}(h_T).
\]
The primary common objective is expected terminal MOCU,
\[
\boxed{
J_{\mathrm{MOCU}}(\pi)
=\mathbb E_{H_T\mid\pi}\!\left[
\operatorname{MOCU}
\bigl(p(\theta\mid H_T)\bigr)\right],
\qquad
\pi^\star=\arg\min_{\pi}J_{\mathrm{MOCU}}(\pi),
}
\label{eq:policy_objective}
\]
subject only to the fixed probe budget
\[
\boxed{
|\xi_{1:T}|=T.
}
\label{eq:fixed_budget}
\]
The expectation is taken over the unknown true system, observation noise, and
any randomization in the probe-selection method. For linear control cost, the Yoon definition gives
\[
\operatorname{MOCU}(h_T)=u_{\mathrm{ctrl}}(h_T)
-\mathbb E[u_\theta^*\mid h_T].
\]
Thus, designs that reduce posterior uncertainty about the minimum safe control
reduce MOCU. Minimizing expected \(u_{\mathrm{ctrl}}\) alone is related but not
identical to minimizing MOCU because the posterior mean of \(u_\theta^*\) also
depends on the policy. Equation~\eqref{eq:belief_mocu} is the primary endpoint;
raw control magnitude and held-out safety are reported separately.

True-system safety remains an important evaluation diagnostic:
\[
P_{\mathrm{safe}}(\pi)
=
\Pr_{\theta^\star,H_T\mid\pi}
\left[
\max_{c\in\mathcal C}
 r_{\mathrm{ctrl}}\!\left(\theta^\star,c,u_{\mathrm{ctrl}}(H_T)\right)\le r_{\max},
\quad
\min_{c\in\mathcal C}
 f_{\mathrm{nadir}}\!\left(\theta^\star,c,u_{\mathrm{ctrl}}(H_T)\right)\ge f_{\min}
\right].
\]
This quantity verifies posterior calibration, control-bank coverage, and
simulator consistency. A method is valid for final comparison only when
\(P_{\mathrm{safe}}(\pi)\ge0.95\); invalid methods receive no rank.

\subsubsection{Sequential probe-selection methods}

The final comparison contains six methods:
\[
\mathcal M
=
\{
\textsc{DAD},
\textsc{RL-sBOED},
\textsc{MoE-sBOED},
\textsc{Myopic},
\textsc{Fixed},
\textsc{Random}
\}.
\]
All six use the same action space, independent reset-to-equilibrium probe bank,
noisy observation model, posterior update, control-requirement bank, Yoon IBR
terminal control selector~\eqref{eq:u_ctrl}, and terminal evaluation. They differ
in how probe designs are chosen and, for the learned components, how policy
improvement is estimated. Table~\ref{tab:methods} summarizes their defining logic.

\label{tab:methods}
\begin{tabular}{@{}llp{3.1cm}p{4.0cm}@{}}

\paragraph{Objective-based Deep Adaptive Design.}

The DAD method uses one adaptive policy trained over the complete \(T\)-probe experiment. At step \(t\), the next probe is selected from the complete accumulated history
\[
h_{t-1}
=
\left\{
(\xi_1,\mathbf y_{\mathrm{obs},1}),
\ldots,
(\xi_{t-1},\mathbf y_{\mathrm{obs},t-1})
\right\},
\]
according to
\[
\boxed{
\xi_t
\sim
\pi_{\phi}
\left(
\cdot\mid h_{t-1}
\right).
}
\label{eq:dad_policy}
\]
Thus, the policy does not use only the latest probe--observation pair. All previous probes and observations are available when the next probe is selected. When repetition is prohibited, probes already contained in \(h_{t-1}\) are masked from the available action set.

The internal history encoder is not prescribed by the objective formulation. The implementation should preserve the existing DAD policy architecture in the current codebase, provided that it processes the complete history \(h_{t-1}\). The main methodological change is the terminal training objective, not the introduction of a new GRU, LSTM, or attention architecture.

The objective-based DAD policy is trained to minimize the terminal engineering action:
\[
\boxed{
J_{\mathrm{DAD}}(\phi)
=
\mathbb E_{\theta^\star,\epsilon_{1:T},H_T\mid\pi_{\phi}}
\left[
\operatorname{MOCU}(H_T)
\right].
}
\label{eq:dad_objective}
\]
This method is non-myopic because the policy is trained using the final consequence of the complete adaptive probe sequence. An early probe is therefore valued not only by its immediate effect, but also by how its observation changes the later probe selections and the final \(u_{\mathrm{ctrl}}\).

Because the probe actions are discrete and the IBR map
\(\psi^\star=\max_{n:w_n>0}\psi_n^\star\) is nondifferentiable, a policy-gradient
estimator is used. For rollout \(b\), the preferred terminal cost is the belief
MOCU~\eqref{eq:belief_mocu} of the sampled trajectory
\[
\left(
\xi_1^{(b)},\ldots,\xi_T^{(b)}
\right);
\]
using raw \(u_{\mathrm{ctrl}}^{(b)}\) is a related proxy. The policy parameters are
updated so that trajectories producing smaller terminal MOCU become more likely.
A learned value baseline and a prespecified entropy regularizer may be used to
reduce gradient variance and discourage premature policy collapse. Checkpoint
selection uses a validation set disjoint from training and final testing. The
primary checkpoint criterion is validation belief MOCU; sequence diversity is
reported as a diagnostic rather than silently changing the engineering objective.

Posterior weights are used to compute the terminal \(u_{\mathrm{ctrl}}(H_T)\), but they do not need to be explicit inputs to the deployed DAD policy. Online probe selection is based on the complete observed history.

\paragraph{RL-sBOED with dense MOCU-reduction rewards.}

RL-sBOED uses the same history representation, action mask, policy class,
posterior update, terminal rule, and optimization budget as DAD. Its purpose is
to isolate the effect of temporal credit assignment. Let
\(m_t=\operatorname{MOCU}(h_t)\) be the belief MOCU after step \(t\).
RL-sBOED uses the dense reward
\[
\boxed{
r_t=m_{t-1}-m_t,\qquad t=1,\ldots,T.
}
\label{eq:rl_sboed_reward}
\]
With undiscounted return,
\[
\sum_{t=1}^{T}r_t=m_0-m_T.
\]
Because \(m_0\) is fixed by the common prior, maximizing expected return is
equivalent to minimizing expected terminal MOCU. Thus, DAD and RL-sBOED
have the same population objective but different credit-assignment signals:
DAD attributes the terminal outcome to the full trajectory, whereas RL-sBOED
exposes the realized stepwise reductions. Any performance difference must
therefore be interpreted as an optimization and credit-assignment effect, not
as a difference in the target engineering objective.

For a fair comparison, both learned methods use matched network capacity,
training simulations, optimizer-update budgets, hyperparameter-selection
budgets, validation systems, and independent training seeds. Their
deterministic argmax policies are used for the primary evaluation; stochastic
rollouts are supplementary sensitivity analyses.

\paragraph{MoE-sBOED: shared-base residual experts.}
\label{sec:moe_sboed}

\paragraph{Motivation from the belief-state control problem.}
Let \(b_t\) denote the posterior belief after history \(h_t\). On the particle
support it is represented by \(b_t=(w_1^{(t)},\ldots,w_N^{(t)})\). Sequential
design is a finite-horizon belief-state Markov decision process:
\begin{align}
V_t(b_t,\mathcal A_t)
&=\min_{a\in\mathcal A_t}Q_t^\star(b_t,a),
\label{eq:moe_bellman_value}\\
Q_t^\star(b_t,a)
&=\mathbb E_{\mathbf y\sim p(\mathbf y\mid b_t,a)}
\left[V_{t+1}\!\left(\mathcal B(b_t,a,\mathbf y),
\mathcal A_t\setminus\{a\}\right)\right],
\label{eq:moe_bellman_q}\\
V_T(b_T,\mathcal A_T)&=\operatorname{MOCU}(b_T),
\label{eq:moe_bellman_terminal}
\end{align}
where \(\mathcal B\) is the Bayesian update and \(\mathcal A_t\) is the
feasible set after masking selected probes. The optimal design is therefore a
mapping from posterior beliefs, not generally a single fixed sequence.

The terminal IBR map is piecewise constant on the particle simplex,
\begin{equation}
u_{\mathrm{ctrl}}(b)
=
\max_{n:\,w_n>0}\psi_{\theta^{(n)}}^\star,
\qquad
\operatorname{MOCU}(b)
=
\psi^\star(b)-\sum_{n=1}^{N}w_n \psi_{\theta^{(n)}}^\star.
\label{eq:moe_piecewise_control}
\end{equation}
Small posterior changes that eliminate or restore a high-\(U\) particle change
both the IBR action and the probe ranking that best resolves control-relevant
uncertainty. The optimal action partitions belief space into regions
\begin{equation}
\mathcal R_a=\left\{b:Q_t^\star(b,a)\le Q_t^\star(b,a'),\
\forall a'\in\mathcal A_t\right\}.
\label{eq:moe_belief_regions}
\end{equation}
This piecewise belief dependence motivates specialized residual corrections on
a shared base policy. It does not imply that a mixture must outperform a
sufficiently expressive, globally optimized single network
(parameter-matched dense ablations remain mandatory).

\paragraph{Shared base and residual experts.}
A shared encoder maps the ordered probe--observation history, posterior
particle set, belief summaries, feasible-action mask, and remaining horizon to
a latent state \(h_t\). MoE-sBOED uses one shared base head and \(E=4\)
independently parameterized residual experts (default). No expert is Fixed,
Myopic, DAD, or RL-sBOED, and no baseline checkpoint initializes the model.
Expert last layers are zero-initialized so training begins as the shared base.
A belief-conditioned router produces
\begin{equation}
g_e(h_t)=\operatorname{softmax}_e(G_\omega(h_t)).
\label{eq:moe_learned_router}
\end{equation}
For top-\(k\) indices \(\mathcal K_k(h_t)\) (default \(k=2\)), renormalized
sparse weights and action logits are
\begin{align}
\widetilde g_e(h_t)&=
\frac{g_e(h_t)\mathbb I[e\in\mathcal K_k(h_t)]}
{\sum_{j\in\mathcal K_k(h_t)}g_j(h_t)},\\
\ell_{\mathrm{MoE}}(h_t,a)&=
\ell_{\mathrm{base}}(h_t,a)
+
s\cdot
\sum_{e\in\mathcal K_k(h_t)}\widetilde g_e(h_t)\,
d_e(h_t,a),\\
\pi_{\mathrm{MoE}}(a\mid h_t)&\propto
\exp\{\ell_{\mathrm{MoE}}(h_t,a)\}
\mathbb I[a\in\mathcal A_t],
\label{eq:moe_policy}
\end{align}
where \(d_e\) is the residual expert correction and
\(s=\operatorname{softplus}(\lambda)\) is a trainable bounded scale.
Top-\(k\) fusion allows a boundary belief to interpolate between adjacent
regimes without cloning an external baseline action.

\paragraph{Optional counterfactual fingerprints and branching.}
The simulator bank can evaluate every feasible next action under a common
latent system (counterfactual utilities). Normalized decision fingerprints
\begin{equation}
z_t(a)=\frac{q_t^{\mathrm{cf}}(a)-
|\mathcal A_t|^{-1}\sum_{a'\in\mathcal A_t}q_t^{\mathrm{cf}}(a')}
{\sqrt{|\mathcal A_t|^{-1}\sum_{a'\in\mathcal A_t}
\left(q_t^{\mathrm{cf}}(a')-\overline q_t^{\mathrm{cf}}\right)^2}+\epsilon}
\label{eq:moe_fingerprint}
\end{equation}
with \(q_t^{\mathrm{cf}}(a)=-\operatorname{MOCU}(\widetilde b_{t+1}^{(a)})\)
(or a stepwise EIG target on the information track) can supervise regime
prototypes and ranking losses when enabled. On the IEEE~5 continuous-duration
objective track the ranking coefficient is typically zero; fingerprints are retained to
power an observation-branching regularizer that penalizes identical routed
decisions when same-step counterfactual rankings disagree.

\paragraph{Training loss and anti-collapse checkpointing.}
Primary optimization is PPO on the telescoping dense reward below, plus weak
router-balance and expert-redundancy penalties. Writing \(\mathcal L_{\mathrm{aux}}\)
for those specialization terms and \(\mathcal L_{\mathrm{branch}}\) for the
optional branching regularizer,
\begin{equation}
\mathcal L_{\mathrm{MoE}}
=
\mathcal L_{\mathrm{PPO}}
+\lambda_{\mathrm{aux}}\mathcal L_{\mathrm{aux}}
+\lambda_{\mathrm{branch}}\mathcal L_{\mathrm{branch}}
+\lambda_{\mathrm{cf}}\mathcal L_{\mathrm{cf}},
\label{eq:moe_training_loss}
\end{equation}
where \(\lambda_{\mathrm{cf}}=0\) is the continuous-duration default. Validation checkpoints
minimize the joint score
\begin{equation}
\operatorname{score}
=
\overline{\mathrm{MOCU}}
-
\lambda_{\mathrm{div}}\cdot
\frac{n_{\mathrm{unique}}}{n_{\mathrm{rollouts}}}
\label{eq:moe_joint_score}
\end{equation}
subject to a soft unique-sequence floor: a diverse checkpoint may replace a
collapsed (\(n_{\mathrm{unique}}\!\approx\!1\)) checkpoint only if its raw MOCU
remains within a configured slack, and a collapsed checkpoint may demote a
diverse one only for a clear MOCU win. This prevents open-loop Fixed-in-disguise
policies without discarding a substantially better engineering objective.

\paragraph{Alignment with terminal MOCU.}
Let \(C_t=u_{\mathrm{ctrl}}(b_t)\). The objective-oriented dense reward
satisfies
\begin{equation}
r_t=C_{t-1}-C_t,\qquad
\sum_{t=1}^{T}r_t=C_0-C_T.
\label{eq:moe_telescoping_reward}
\end{equation}
Because \(C_0\) and the distribution of
\(u_{\mathrm{ctrl,opt}}(\theta^\star)\) are common to every policy,
maximizing expected undiscounted return is equivalent to minimizing expected
terminal MOCU. The mixture changes policy approximation, not the scientific
endpoint.

\paragraph{Transfer domain (SIR ODE).}
To rebut power-grid specificity, the same amortized methods are evaluated on a
classical sequential BOED task matching Ivanova et al.~iDAD Appendix~D.6:
population \(N=500\), \(I_0=2\), time grid
\(\texttt{linspace}(0,100,10000)\), log-normal prior on \((\beta,\gamma)\),
design \(\xi=\tau\) (strictly increasing), and observation of the infected
count with an explicit Gaussian likelihood
\(y\sim\mathcal N\bigl(I(\tau;\theta),\sigma_y^2\bigr)\) (ODE drift only;
Foster et al.~DAD / sPCE). Implicit SIR SDE path likelihoods (InfoNCE/NWJ)
are out of scope for this estimator class.

\paragraph{Scope of the theoretical argument.}
If belief space contains regimes on which specialists have uniform value
errors \(\epsilon_e\), and routing assigns the wrong regime with probability
at most \(\delta\), a standard plug-in decision argument gives the indicative
regret decomposition
\begin{equation}
\mathbb E[Q_t^\star(b_t,\widehat a_t)-V_t(b_t)]
\le 2\max_e\epsilon_e+\delta\,\Delta_{\max},
\label{eq:moe_regret_decomposition}
\end{equation}
where \(\Delta_{\max}\) bounds an action-value gap. The expression explains
what must improve---local approximation and routing---but is not a guarantee
that MoE beats every baseline. If the same action minimizes
Eq.~\eqref{eq:moe_bellman_q} throughout the reachable belief set, Fixed is
already optimal and no adaptive architecture can obtain a strict gain.

The MoE mechanism is therefore supported only if experiments separately show
\begin{enumerate}
    \item lower paired held-out terminal MOCU than RL-sBOED and a
    parameter-matched single network, with safety rate \(\ge0.95\);
    \item adaptivity evidence (\(n_{\mathrm{unique}}\gg 1\), nontrivial
    router/expert utilization) on claimed adaptive cells; and
    \item transfer to the SIR ODE EIG benchmark under the same method list.
\end{enumerate}
Without these controls, an observed gain can be attributed to extra capacity,
computation, collapse to Fixed, or random variation rather than the MoE
mechanism.

\paragraph{Myopic control-oriented design.}

The Myopic method is adaptive but evaluates only the immediate consequence of the next probe. At history \(h_{t-1}\), the posterior-predictive distribution for candidate \(\xi\in\Xi_t\) is
\[
\boxed{
p(\mathbf y\mid h_{t-1},\xi)
=
\sum_{n=1}^{N}
w_n^{(t-1)}
\mathcal N\!\left(
\mathbf y;\mathbf Y_{n,\xi},
\sigma_y^2\mathbf I_{N_{\mathrm{obs}}}
\right),
}
\label{eq:predictive_y}
\]
where
\(\mathbf Y_{n,\xi}=\mathbf y_{\mathrm{clean}}(\theta^{(n)},\xi)\).
For a hypothetical observation vector \(\mathbf y\), the one-step posterior
weights are
\[
w_n^{+}(\xi,\mathbf y)
=
\frac{
w_n^{(t-1)}
\mathcal N\!\left(
\mathbf y;\mathbf Y_{n,\xi},
\sigma_y^2\mathbf I_{N_{\mathrm{obs}}}
\right)
}{
\sum_{m=1}^{N}
w_m^{(t-1)}
\mathcal N\!\left(
\mathbf y;\mathbf Y_{m,\xi},
\sigma_y^2\mathbf I_{N_{\mathrm{obs}}}
\right)
}.
\]
The IBR action that would be selected immediately after that hypothetical observation is
\[
\psi^{\star+}(\xi,\mathbf y)
=
\max_{n:\,w_n^{+}(\xi,\mathbf y)>0}\psi_n^\star.
\]
The one-step score is
\[
\boxed{
J_{\mathrm{myopic}}(\xi\mid h_{t-1})
=
\mathbb E_{\mathbf y\mid h_{t-1},\xi}
\left[
\psi^{\star+}(\xi,\mathbf y)
-
\sum_{n=1}^{N}
w_n^{+}(\xi,\mathbf y)\,\psi_n^\star
\right],
}
\label{eq:myopic_score}
\]
and the selected probe is
\[
\boxed{
\xi_t^{\mathrm{myopic}}
=
\arg\min_{\xi\in\Xi_t}
J_{\mathrm{myopic}}(\xi\mid h_{t-1}).
}
\label{eq:myopic_action}
\]
Equivalently, the method minimizes the expected one-step belief MOCU. When
only the IBR magnitude is used as a proxy score, the expected immediate decrease
\[
\Delta u_{\mathrm{myopic}}(\xi\mid h_{t-1})
=
\psi^\star(h_{t-1})
-
\mathbb E_{\mathbf y\mid h_{t-1},\xi}
\bigl[
\psi^{\star+}(\xi,\mathbf y)
\bigr]
\]
is maximized instead.

The expectation in Eq.~\eqref{eq:myopic_score} is evaluated by posterior-predictive Monte Carlo. For sample \(r\), draw
\[
n_r\sim\operatorname{Categorical}
\left(w_1^{(t-1)},\ldots,w_N^{(t-1)}\right),
\qquad
\mathbf y_r=\mathbf Y_{n_r,\xi}+\boldsymbol\epsilon_r,
\qquad
\boldsymbol\epsilon_r
\sim
\mathcal N\!\left(
\mathbf 0,\sigma_y^2\mathbf I_{N_{\mathrm{obs}}}
\right),
\]
and approximate
\[
\widehat J_{\mathrm{myopic}}(\xi\mid h_{t-1})
=
\frac{1}{R}
\sum_{r=1}^{R}
u_{\mathrm{ctrl}}^{+}(\xi,\mathbf y_r).
\]
Common random numbers should be used across candidate probes when feasible. The method remains genuinely myopic because it evaluates only the next posterior and ignores the effect of the remaining \(T-t\) future probes. Since the IBR action is a discrete maximum over particle requirements, ties across probe candidates can occur; they are resolved by a fixed deterministic action-index rule unless a secondary tie-breaker is explicitly reported.

\paragraph{Optimized fixed design.}

The Fixed method is nonadaptive: all \(T\) probes are chosen before any
observations are available. Under the primary continuous-duration track,
probes are independent and exact repetition is prohibited, so an unordered
duration subset is the natural Fixed object
\[
\mathfrak X_T
=
\left\{
\{d_1,\ldots,d_T\}\subset\mathcal D:
d_i\ne d_j\ \text{for}\ i\ne j
\right\}.
\]
The optimized fixed design is
\[
\boxed{
\mathcal X_{\mathrm{fixed}}^{\star}
=
\arg\min_{\mathcal X\in\mathfrak X_T}
\mathbb E_{\theta^\star,y_{\mathcal X}\mid\mathcal X}
\left[
\operatorname{MOCU}(H_T;\mathcal X)
\right].
}
\label{eq:fixed_objective}
\]
For \(B_{\mathrm{fix}}\) offline Monte Carlo rollouts,
\[
\widehat J_{\mathrm{fix}}(\mathcal X)
=
\frac{1}{B_{\mathrm{fix}}}
\sum_{b=1}^{B_{\mathrm{fix}}}
\operatorname{MOCU}\!\left(H_T^{(b)};\mathcal X\right),
\]
and the tuple with the smallest estimate is selected. No fixed design is removed for producing a large control; a large value simply gives that design a worse objective score.

With catalog size \(N_\xi=|\mathcal D|\) from Eq.~\eqref{eq:design_space},
the number of nonrepeated duration subsets is
\[
\boxed{
|\mathfrak X_T|
=
\binom{N_\xi}{T}.
}
\label{eq:fixed_space_size}
\]
For the IEEE~5 continuous-duration setting (\(N_\xi=400\)),
\[
|\mathfrak X_T|
=
\binom{400}{T},
\]
so exhaustive Fixed search is impractical for the primary horizons
\(T\in\{2,3,4,5\}\). A documented approximation such as beam search, coarse
duration subsample, or Monte Carlo subset search must be used and reported.
A canonical sorted order is used only to store and report the selected subset;
conditional independence makes its execution order irrelevant for Fixed.

\paragraph{Random reference.}

The Random method samples uniformly without replacement from the currently available probes:
\[
\boxed{
\pi_{\mathrm{random}}(\xi^a\mid h_{t-1})
=
\frac{m_t(a)}{
\sum_{a'=1}^{N_\xi}m_t(a')
}.
}
\label{eq:random_policy}
\]
Sequential uniform sampling without replacement induces a uniform distribution
over size-\(T\) duration subsets.
Random uses the same posterior update and terminal control rule as the other methods. It does not optimize Eq.~\eqref{eq:policy_objective}; it is included only as an uninformed reference showing whether objective-guided probe selection improves the final engineering action beyond chance selection.

\subsection{7. Reproducibility, Computation, and Statistical Evaluation}

\subsubsection{Offline computation}

Two independent offline simulation banks support all methods. The probe-response bank stores
\[
\mathbf Y_{n,a}
=
\mathbf y_{\mathrm{clean}}(\theta^{(n)},\xi^a)
\]
for particle \(n\) and candidate probe \(a\). It is generated only from probe simulations and is used to evaluate likelihoods, posterior-predictive distributions, hypothetical Myopic updates, and DAD training rollouts.

The operator-cost bank stores the Yoon model-specific optima
\[
\psi_n^\star=\psi_{\theta^{(n)}}^\star=\max_{c\in\mathcal C}u_{\mathrm{req}}(\theta^{(n)},c),
\]
computed only from separate terminal control simulations. Every \(\psi_n^\star\) must be finite under Eq.~\eqref{eq:control_feasibility}. Compatibility between \(\mathbf Y_{n,a}\) and \(\psi_n^\star\) is verified using particle values, particle ordering, network configuration, simulator version, control scenario, and units rather than by array shape alone.

\subsubsection{Common evaluation protocol}

Each method is evaluated on the same independently sampled test systems,
prior particles, observation-noise model, terminal contingency set, probe
horizon \(T\), and control thresholds. Test systems and noise streams are
paired across methods to reduce Monte Carlo variance. Training, validation,
calibration, and final test systems are mutually disjoint; final test outcomes
are not used for model selection or hyperparameter tuning. For each rollout,
the method produces a history \(H_T\), the common posterior update yields
\(w_{1:N}^{(T)}\), and the robust rule~\eqref{eq:particle_u_ctrl}
produces the terminal action. The selected \(u_{\mathrm{ctrl}}(H_T)\) is evaluated on the true
parameter \(\theta^\star\), not on a posterior mean or on a particle selected
with knowledge of \(\theta^\star\).

The primary comparison metric is empirical MOCU averaged over held-out rollouts,
\[
\boxed{
\widehat{\operatorname{MOCU}}_m
=
\frac{1}{B_{\mathrm{test}}}
\sum_{b=1}^{B_{\mathrm{test}}}
\operatorname{MOCU}\!\left(
p(\theta\mid H_T^{(b)})\right),
\qquad
m\in\mathcal M,
}
\]
which estimates~\eqref{eq:belief_mocu}. The realized true-system excess control
\[
u_{\mathrm{ctrl}}(H_T)-u_{\theta^\star}^*
\]
are reported as secondary diagnostics. Among methods that satisfy the
prespecified safety requirement, lower MOCU is better. Mean terminal \(\psi^\star\)
(equivalently \(u_{\mathrm{ctrl}}\)) remains a secondary physical-control
metric. No method is ranked unless it first satisfies the safety requirement;
the MOCU components and cost--risk frontier accompany the scalar mean.

The true-system safety rate
\[
\widehat P_{\mathrm{safe},m}
=
\frac{1}{B_{\mathrm{test}}}
\sum_{b=1}^{B_{\mathrm{test}}}
\mathbf 1\{\text{terminal control is safe on }\theta^{\star(b)}\}
\]
is reported as a validation diagnostic. A method with \(\widehat P_{\mathrm{safe},m}<0.95\) is marked invalid and is not ranked. The numerator and denominator (for example, \(61/64\)) and a binomial confidence interval are reported with the point estimate. The final test size is selected by an a priori precision or power calculation; a small pilot test set is not used to make a definitive 0.95 safety claim. A low value indicates posterior miscalibration, insufficient particle support, an incomplete control range, or simulator mismatch; it does not imply that the diagnostic probe itself was unsafe. Posterior entropy and terminal EIG may be reported as secondary explanatory measures, but they do not determine the method ranking.

\subsubsection{Publication-level experimental design}

\paragraph{Prespecified systems and factors.}
The primary benchmark is a factorial comparison over IEEE
\(\{5,9,14\}\)-bus systems and horizons \(T\in\{2,3,4,5\}\). The terminal
control grid, IBR terminal rule, observation model,
uncertainty distribution, and evaluation set are held fixed across horizons
within each system. At least three independent training seeds are used for
each learned method and setting; more are preferred when between-seed
variation is material. All methods receive matched simulation and tuning
budgets. Results from failed or collapsed seeds are reported rather than
discarded post hoc.

\paragraph{Primary hypotheses.}
The hypotheses are directional and are declared before examining final test
results:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \(H_1\): a full-horizon learned policy reduces paired MOCU relative
    to Myopic while satisfying the safety requirement;
    \item \(H_2\): an adaptive policy reduces paired MOCU relative to the
    optimized Fixed design, demonstrating value from history-dependent
    branching rather than only offline subset optimization;
    \item \(H_3\): the advantage over Myopic, if present, increases with
    independently measured non-myopic opportunity and is not inferred merely
    from a larger horizon;
    \item \(H_4\): dense credit assignment changes optimization reliability,
    measured by RL-sBOED versus DAD across matched seeds, without changing the
    target terminal objective.
\end{enumerate}
Failure to reject these hypotheses is a valid scientific result and must not
be repaired by redefining the endpoint after evaluation.

\paragraph{Non-myopic opportunity audit.}
Changing prose alone cannot make a full-horizon method superior. Because all
probe simulations are physically independent conditional on \(\theta\), the
benchmark does not rely on one probe changing the physical outcome of another.
It must instead contain physically defensible uncertainty structures in which
an early observation changes the posterior ranking of later measurement
choices. Before policy training, the candidate
probe bank is audited for:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \emph{control-relevant ambiguity}: distinct parameter regions
    produce similar responses to an early probe but require different
    terminal controls;
    \item \emph{probe complementarity}: a pair or sequence resolves such
    ambiguity more effectively than either probe alone;
    \item \emph{history-dependent measurement branching}: the
    posterior-optimal second bus--amplitude measurement differs across
    plausible first-probe outcomes even though its physical response model is
    unchanged;
    \item \emph{nondegenerate noise}: observations are neither essentially
    deterministic nor so noisy that no method can learn.
\end{enumerate}
These properties are quantified from the prior and simulation bank without
using final test labels. If they are absent, Myopic parity is the expected
outcome and should be reported honestly. If challenge regimes are added, they
must correspond to defensible operating uncertainties or sensor conditions
and must be reported alongside, not substituted for, the base benchmark.

\paragraph{Statistical analysis.}
For every method pair, the unit of pairing is the same held-out true system
and, where applicable, the same noise realization. The primary contrast is
\[
\Delta_{m,\mathrm{Myopic}}
=
\widehat{\operatorname{MOCU}}_m
-
\widehat{\operatorname{MOCU}}_{\mathrm{Myopic}}.
\]
A paired bootstrap confidence interval is reported for each prespecified
contrast. An advantage is claimed only when the method is valid and the
confidence interval for \(\Delta\) lies below zero. Effect sizes, confidence
intervals, and exact test counts are reported in addition to ranks. Familywise
or false-discovery-rate control is used for confirmatory claims across the
system--horizon matrix; unadjusted analyses are labeled exploratory.

\paragraph{Adaptivity and optimization diagnostics.}
For DAD and RL-sBOED, the number of unique deterministic action sequences,
sequence entropy, action frequencies by step, and conditional second-action
maps are reported. A policy that produces one sequence across held-out
histories is described as effectively open-loop and cannot support a claim of
adaptive experimental design. Training and validation learning curves,
between-seed dispersion, and the selected checkpoint rule are included so
that a negative result can be separated from policy-optimization failure.

\paragraph{Required ablations.}
The publication study includes: terminal-only versus dense rewards under the
same policy class; history-conditioned versus history-ablated policies;
deterministic versus stochastic evaluation as a sensitivity analysis; matched
training-budget curves; observation-noise sensitivity; particle-count
sensitivity; and an oracle or exhaustive small-design calculation where
tractable. These ablations test whether any gain comes from sequential
planning, adaptive branching, additional computation, or an implementation
artifact.

\subsubsection{End-to-end computational procedures}

Algorithms~\ref{alg:banks}--\ref{alg:evaluation} summarize the complete implementation. The same posterior and terminal control routines are used by every method.

\begin{algorithm}[htbp]
\caption{Offline construction of shared probe and control banks}
\label{alg:banks}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Particles \(\{\theta^{(n)}\}_{n=1}^{N}\); probe set \(\Xi=\{\xi^a\}_{a=1}^{N_\xi}\); ordered support set \(\mathcal S=\{u^1<\cdots<u^{N_u}\}\); safety limits \(r_{\max}\), \(f_{\min}\)
\Ensure Probe bank \(Y\), finite operator-cost bank \(\{\psi_n^\star\}\), and validated metadata
\For{\(n=1,\ldots,N\)}
    \For{\(a=1,\ldots,N_\xi\)}
        \State Reset the probe simulator to the common equilibrium \(x_0\)
        \State Set \(u_{\mathrm{ctrl}}\gets0\) and apply only probe \(\xi^a\)
        \State Store \(\mathbf Y_{n,a}\gets\mathbf y_{\mathrm{clean}}(\theta^{(n)},\xi^a)\)
    \EndFor
    \State Reset the terminal control simulator and set \(P_{\mathrm{probe}}\gets0\)
    \State Compute \(u_{\mathrm{req}}(\theta^{(n)},c)\) for every \(c\in\mathcal C\)
    \State Store \(\psi_n^\star\gets\max_{c\in\mathcal C}u_{\mathrm{req}}(\theta^{(n)},c)\)
    \If{no candidate control is safe}
        \State Stop with a configuration error and enlarge \(\mathcal S\)
    \EndIf
\EndFor
\State Validate particle identity, ordering, network, scenario, simulator version, and units
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\begin{algorithm}[htbp]
\caption{Preparation of DAD, RL-sBOED, MoE-sBOED, Myopic, Fixed, and Random methods}
\label{alg:method_preparation}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Shared banks \(Y,\{\psi_n^\star\}\), prior particles and weights, probe horizon \(T\)
\Ensure Trained DAD, RL-sBOED, and MoE-sBOED policies, optimized Fixed subset, and configured Myopic/Random methods
\For{each learned method \(m\in\{\textsc{DAD},\textsc{RL-sBOED}\}\) and training seed}
\State Initialize policy parameters \(\phi_m\), value baseline, and action mask
\Repeat
    \For{\(b=1,\ldots,B\)}
        \State Sample \(\theta^{\star(b)}\) and initialize empty history and action mask
        \For{\(t=1,\ldots,T\)}
            \State Provide \(h_{t-1}^{(b)}\) to policy \(m\) and sample \(\xi_t^{(b)}\sim\pi_{\phi_m}(\cdot\mid h_{t-1}^{(b)})\)
            \State Generate a noisy probe observation and append it to the history
            \State Compute \(u_t^{(b)}=u_{\mathrm{ctrl}}(h_t^{(b)})\)
        \EndFor
        \State Assign terminal cost for DAD or dense rewards \(u_{t-1}^{(b)}-u_t^{(b)}\) for RL-sBOED
    \EndFor
    \State Update \(\phi_m\) with the prespecified policy-gradient routine
\Until{the training budget or stopping criterion is satisfied}
\State Select the checkpoint using disjoint validation mean \(u_{\mathrm{ctrl}}\)
\EndFor
\For{each subset \(\mathcal X\in\mathfrak X_T\)}
    \State Estimate \(\widehat J_{\mathrm{fix}}(\mathcal X)\) using complete \(T\)-probe rollouts
\EndFor
\State Select \(\mathcal X_{\mathrm{fixed}}^{\star}=\arg\min_{\mathcal X}\widehat J_{\mathrm{fix}}(\mathcal X)\)
\State Configure Myopic using Eq.~\eqref{eq:myopic_action}
\State Configure Random using Eq.~\eqref{eq:random_policy}
\State Initialize MoE-sBOED shared base + residual experts (zero-init residuals)
\State Train MoE-sBOED with Eq.~\eqref{eq:moe_training_loss} (PPO; optional CF/branching)
\State Select MoE checkpoint by joint score Eq.~\eqref{eq:moe_joint_score} with soft unique floor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\begin{algorithm}[htbp]
\caption{Unified rollout and evaluation for all six methods}
\label{alg:evaluation}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Method \(m\in\{\textsc{DAD},\textsc{RL-sBOED},\textsc{MoE-sBOED},\textsc{Myopic},\textsc{Fixed},\textsc{Random}\}\); true system \(\theta^\star\); shared banks \(Y,\{\psi_n^\star\}\); \(T,\sigma_y^2\)
\Ensure Terminal \(\psi^\star\) (\(u_{\mathrm{ctrl}}\)), belief MOCU, true-system diagnostic, and rollout record
\State Initialize \(h_0\gets\varnothing\), posterior weights \(w^{(0)}\), and available set \(\Xi_1\gets\Xi\)
\For{\(t=1,\ldots,T\)}
    \If{\(m\in\{\textsc{DAD},\textsc{RL-sBOED}\}\)}
        \State Select \(\xi_t\) from the trained policy using the complete history \(h_{t-1}\)
    \ElsIf{\(m=\textsc{MoE-sBOED}\)}
        \State Select \(\xi_t\) using the fused score in Eq.~\eqref{eq:moe_policy}
    \ElsIf{\(m=\textsc{Myopic}\)}
        \State Evaluate Eq.~\eqref{eq:myopic_score} for every \(\xi\in\Xi_t\) and choose the minimum
    \ElsIf{\(m=\textsc{Fixed}\)}
        \State Select the next element of the canonically ordered \(\mathcal X_{\mathrm{fixed}}^{\star}\)
    \Else
        \State Sample \(\xi_t\) uniformly from \(\Xi_t\)
    \EndIf
    \State Reset the probe simulator to \(x_0\), set \(u_{\mathrm{ctrl}}\gets0\), and apply \(\xi_t\)
    \State Generate \(\mathbf y_{\mathrm{obs},t}=\mathbf y_{\mathrm{clean}}(\theta^\star,\xi_t)+\boldsymbol\epsilon_t\)
    \State Update posterior weights and append \((\xi_t,\mathbf y_{\mathrm{obs},t})\) to the history
    \State Remove \(\xi_t\) from \(\Xi_t\) when repetition is prohibited
\EndFor
\State Compute \(u_{\mathrm{ctrl}}(H_T)\) using Eq.~\eqref{eq:u_ctrl}
\State Reset the terminal control simulator, set \(P_{\mathrm{probe}}\gets0\), and apply only \(u_{\mathrm{ctrl}}(H_T)\)
\State Record terminal control, posterior coverage, true-system safety, and excess control
\State \Return rollout results
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The algorithms preserve the central engineering distinction: the probes are diagnostic tests, while \(u_{\mathrm{ctrl}}\) is the consequential terminal intervention. Poor probe choices remain admissible but generally lead to a more conservative final action.

\paragraph{Practical problem statement.}
Consider an IEEE test system with an unknown but time-invariant parameter
vector \(\theta=(\mathbf M,\mathbf K)\). Before choosing one scalar
supplementary-control magnitude, the operator may perform \(T\) sequential,
independent diagnostic probes of adaptively selected, nonrepeated duration at a
fixed bus and amplitude. The selected magnitude must protect the system
against every contingency in a prespecified N--1 set \(\mathcal C\), so the
particle-specific Yoon model-optimal operator is
\[
\psi_\theta^\star=\max_{c\in\mathcal C}u_{\mathrm{req}}(\theta,c).
\]

Different durations stress the frequency response differently. A sampled
max-|ROCOF| observation can therefore change which posterior particles
determine \(\psi^\star=\max_n\psi_n^\star\), making a
different remaining probe most useful. Myopic selects the probe with the
smallest expected one-step belief MOCU, whereas DAD and RL-sBOED optimize the
complete observation-contingent sequence; the experiment tests whether planning
across these shifts reduces held-out MOCU.

All methods use the same \(\theta\) distribution, probe-response bank,
observation noise, posterior update, terminal control rule, and paired
held-out systems. The primary endpoint is mean control-oriented MOCU
among methods satisfying
the prespecified safety requirement. A full-horizon advantage is claimed only
when it is reproduced across training seeds, its paired confidence interval
against Myopic lies below zero, and the learned deterministic policy exhibits
genuine history-dependent branching rather than a single open-loop sequence.
