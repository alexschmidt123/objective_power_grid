\begin{abstract}
Learning uncertain dynamic parameters is important for reliable power-grid
operation. Existing experimental-design studies commonly maximize information
gain, although greater parameter information does not necessarily improve a
downstream control decision. This paper formulates power-grid probing as an
objective-driven sequential Bayesian experimental-design problem. Each
experiment selects a disturbance location and duration, observes the resulting
frequency response, updates uncertainty over machine-specific dynamic
parameters, and chooses a safety-aware control action. Expected information
gain (EIG) and mean objective cost of uncertainty (MOCU) are studied as distinct
design objectives. Fixed, random, myopic, deep adaptive design (DAD),
reinforcement-learning sequential Bayesian optimal experimental design
(RL-sBOED), and Step-DAD policies are evaluated under a common held-out
protocol. A nonlinear susceptible--infected--recovered (SIR) model is used to
validate the BOED implementations, while IEEE 9-bus and IEEE 14-bus systems
evaluate control-oriented design, adaptivity, non-myopic planning, safety, and
computational cost. \todo{Insert final quantitative results after all seeds and

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}
\IEEEPARstart{I}{ncreasing} penetration of inverter-based resources makes
power-system dynamic parameters more uncertain and potentially time varying.
Reliable estimates of inertia, damping, and related dynamic quantities are
important for frequency-security assessment and control selection.
\todo{Add citations on low-inertia grids and dynamic-parameter uncertainty.}

Active probing provides an opportunity to learn these quantities by applying a
controlled disturbance and measuring the resulting frequency response. Such an
experiment raises several design questions: where should the disturbance be
applied, how long should it last, and should later experiments depend on earlier
observations? A fixed sequence cannot respond to the realized system, whereas
an adaptive policy can use the accumulating observation history.

Most Bayesian experimental-design formulations value an experiment through
expected information gain. EIG is appropriate for parameter identification,
but information that reduces total parameter uncertainty is not necessarily
the information most relevant to a downstream control decision. Different
parameter values can imply the same control action, while small uncertainty in
a decision-sensitive direction can materially change the required safe
control. This distinction motivates objective-driven experimental design.

This work formulates power-grid probing as a sequential Bayesian experimental-
design problem over discrete disturbance buses and durations. In addition to
EIG, we consider a safety-aware MOCU objective that values an experiment by the
expected reduction in the opportunity cost of the final control decision. The
framework therefore connects physical probing, Bayesian posterior inference,
and operational control within one evaluation protocol.

The main contributions are as follows:
\begin{itemize}
    \item We formulate power-grid dynamic probing as sequential BOED over
    disturbance locations and durations.
    \item We define a control-oriented design objective based on safety-aware
    MOCU, connecting observations to a downstream safe control decision.
    \item We adapt and compare random, fixed, myopic, DAD, RL-sBOED, and
    Step-DAD under common banks, posterior inference, held-out systems, and
    evaluation noise.
    \item We investigate objective choice, adaptivity, non-myopic behavior,
    safety, horizon, observation noise, computational cost, and scalability on
    an SIR benchmark and IEEE power-grid systems.
\end{itemize}

\section{Problem Formulation}
\label{sec:problem}
\subsection{Power-System Dynamics and Uncertainty}
For each retained dynamic machine $i$, consider
\begin{align}
    \dot{\delta}_i(t) &= \omega_i(t), \\
    M_i\dot{\omega}_i(t)
    &= P_{m,i}-P_{e,i}(\boldsymbol{\delta}(t))
       -K_i\omega_i(t)+p_i(t;d).
\end{align}
Here, $\delta_i$ and $\omega_i$ are rotor-angle and frequency deviations,
$M_i$ is inertia, $K_i$ is a machine-specific damping or response coefficient,
and $p_i(t;d)$ is the designed probe. Define
\begin{equation}
    \boldsymbol{\theta}=(M_1,\ldots,M_G,K_1,\ldots,K_G).
\end{equation}
For IEEE14, dynamic machines are retained only at buses 1, 2, 3, 6, and 8;
therefore, the uncertain vector is 10-dimensional. Non-dynamic buses are not
assigned artificial $M_i$ or $K_i$. \todo{State and justify all prior ranges.}

\subsection{Experimental Action Space}
At step $t$,
\begin{equation}
    d_t=(b_t,\tau_t)\in\mathcal D,
\end{equation}
where $b_t$ is the disturbance bus and $\tau_t$ is its duration. With six
durations and $B$ buses, $|\mathcal D|=6B$. IEEE9 therefore has 54 actions and
IEEE14 has 84. \todo{List the frozen, independently audited duration sets.}

\subsection{Observation and Posterior Update}
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf y_t=h(\boldsymbol{\theta},d_t)+\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_t,
    \qquad \boldsymbol{\epsilon}_t\sim
    \mathcal N(\mathbf 0,\sigma^2\mathbf I).
\end{equation}
The main power-grid protocol retains $N_{\mathrm{obs}}=5$ evenly spaced
frequency-response samples. Define
\begin{equation}
    h_t=\{(d_1,\mathbf y_1),\ldots,(d_t,\mathbf y_t)\}.
\end{equation}
Then
\begin{equation}
    p(\boldsymbol{\theta}\mid h_t)\propto
    p(\mathbf y_t\mid\boldsymbol{\theta},d_t)
    p(\boldsymbol{\theta}\mid h_{t-1}).
\end{equation}

\subsection{Sequential Design Policy}
\begin{equation}
    d_t=\pi_t(h_{t-1}),\qquad t=1,\ldots,T.
\end{equation}
A policy is adaptive if later actions vary with observations. It is non-myopic
if it accounts for effects on multiple future decisions rather than optimizing
only the next immediate gain.

\section{Design Objectives}
\label{sec:objectives}
\subsection{Expected Information Gain}
\begin{equation}
\operatorname{EIG}(d\mid h)=\E_{\mathbf y\mid d,h}
\left[\Dkl\!\left(p(\boldsymbol{\theta}\mid h,d,\mathbf y)
\,\|\,p(\boldsymbol{\theta}\mid h)\right)\right].
\end{equation}
EIG values parameter learning and does not directly encode the final control
decision. Higher EIG is better.

\subsection{Safety-Aware Control Decision}
Let $u\in\mathcal U$ denote a control magnitude and
$C(u,\boldsymbol{\theta})$ its safety-aware cost. Define
\begin{align}
u^*(\boldsymbol{\theta})
&=\arg\min_{u\in\mathcal U}C(u,\boldsymbol{\theta}),\\
u^*(h_t)
&=\arg\min_{u\in\mathcal U}\E_{\boldsymbol{\theta}\mid h_t}
[C(u,\boldsymbol{\theta})].
\end{align}
\todo{State the frequency-security constraint, control grid, safety quantile,
and penalties exactly as implemented.}

\subsection{MOCU Objective}
\begin{align}
\ocu(\boldsymbol{\theta},h_t)
&=C(u^*(h_t),\boldsymbol{\theta})
-C(u^*(\boldsymbol{\theta}),\boldsymbol{\theta}),\\
\mocu(h_t)
&=\E_{\boldsymbol{\theta}\mid h_t}
[\ocu(\boldsymbol{\theta},h_t)].
\end{align}
Lower MOCU is better. MOCU equal to zero means posterior uncertainty creates no
regret relative to the clairvoyant control. The central objective is
\begin{equation}
\pi^*=\arg\min_{\pi}\E_{\pi}[\mocu(h_T)].
\label{eq:main-objective}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Random Design}
Random selects feasible actions without using the history and is averaged over
repeated random sequences.

\subsection{Fixed Open-Loop Design}
Fixed chooses the complete sequence before observing data,
\begin{equation}
d_{1:T}^{\mathrm{fixed}}=\arg\min_{d_{1:T}}\E[\mocu(h_T)].
\end{equation}
The same sequence is used for every system. Observations update the posterior
and final control but cannot alter later experiments. ``Fixed MOCU'' is the
same terminal metric achieved by the fixed policy, not a separate objective.

\subsection{Myopic Design}
Myopic recomputes the best immediate action after each observation but does not
explicitly value later design opportunities.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
Organize the discussion around these questions:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item When does observation-dependent adaptation improve control?
    \item When is a strong fixed design competitive?
    \item When does non-myopic planning outperform myopic selection?
    \item Does EIG improvement translate to lower MOCU?
    \item Does increasing $T$ cause information or decision saturation?
    \item Is extra online computation justified by improvement?
    \item How do action count and latent dimension affect scalability?
\end{enumerate}
A negative result is informative. For example, a competitive fixed design can
indicate limited posterior-dependent decision branching.

\section{Limitations and Future Work}
\label{sec:limitations}
The study uses simulated IEEE systems, prescribed priors, Gaussian noise, fixed
probe amplitude, discrete actions, and a simplified downstream control. Three
training seeds provide a limited estimate of optimizer variability. Future work
should consider larger systems, correlated and time-varying priors, multiple
amplitudes, PMU channel selection, higher-fidelity constraints, and real or
hardware-in-the-loop validation.
